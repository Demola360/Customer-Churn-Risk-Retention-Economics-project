# Recommendations

If I was presenting this to the business, here's what I'd actually tell them to do.
Contacting this specific list returns over £10 for every £1 spent

**Go after High Risk / High Value first, and treat everyone else as a lower priority, not an equal one.** That group is 2,218 customers out of just over 7,000, and they're carrying £197,836.70 in monthly revenue between them. Just over half of them will churn if nothing changes. That's not a guess, it's what the model predicted almost exactly matching what actually happened in the historical data. Contacting the top 250 of them, ranked by expected value rather than just by risk, would be worth about £63,144 after covering the cost of reaching out to them. That's the number I'd lead with in any meeting about this.

**Don't spread the budget evenly across everyone flagged as "at risk."** Low Risk / Low Value customers actually lose money on average if you contact them, the cost of reaching out is higher than what you'd expect to get back. So the recommendation isn't "contact more people," it's "contact the right people," and the data is specific about who that is.

**The three things actually driving churn, in order of how much they matter:**

Contract type is the biggest lever by far. Month to month customers churn at 42.7%, two year contract customers churn at 2.8%. If there's one thing worth testing as an intervention, it's offering month to month customers an incentive to move onto a longer contract, since that alone would likely do more than any individual retention call.

Add on services matter too, and this one's actionable in a different way. Customers without online security or tech support churn at more than double the rate of customers with both. Bundling these in, or pushing them harder at signup, is a cheap lever compared to a full retention campaign.

Payment method is the third one, and it's worth flagging because it's less obvious. Electronic check customers churn worse than every other payment method, in every contract tier, not just on average. That's likely more about the kind of customer who ends up on that payment method than the payment method itself, but it's still a useful, cheap signal to add into any targeting.

**What I'd actually recommend doing next, not just what the data shows:** run a real pilot on a few hundred customers from the High Risk / High Value group, hold back a control group who don't get contacted even though they qualify, and measure the actual difference in churn after 90 days. Everything in this project up to that point is analysis. That pilot is the only way to turn the £27.50 cost and 32.5% success rate I assumed into real, measured numbers instead of educated guesses.

**One thing I'd be upfront about if asked.** The £63,144 figure and everything built on top of it assumes retaining a customer is worth 12 months of their revenue. I didn't test that assumption against alternatives the way I tested the cost and success rate ranges, and it's probably the single number in this whole project that would move the final figure the most if it turned out to be wrong. Worth saying that out loud rather than letting the confidence of one number hide the fact that it's still an assumption.
